Strand 2 · Understanding enterprise

Who are you up against?
How could you grow?
Is it worth it?

Enterprise is not static. A business must know its competitors and what gives it the edge, then choose how to adapt or expand: new products, new markets, new partners, new technology. Before it commits, it weighs the costs against the benefits, and when the world around it changes it adjusts its marketing mix or its business model.

Learning outcomes

Students learn about:

  • the importance of identifying potential competition and competitors and the significance of a business’ competitive advantage and how a business can capitalise on this.
  • how enterprise is not static and how a business may develop and may use technology to support growth.
  • the importance of considering both potential benefits and potential costs when making business decisions.
  • how a business may have to adapt to external drivers (including customer demographics, competition, and economic factors) to enhance financial sustainability, and how the business can address issues through considering their product/service, pricing, promotion, people, packaging, process, and place and/or business model.

Porter’s five forces model, from the specification's glossary: "Porter’s model includes five forces which support deeper understanding of the competitive forces within business which drive how economic value is divided amongst stakeholders. The five forces with Porter’s model include the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of suppliers, the bargaining power of customers, the threat of substitutes and competitive rivalry." The marketing mix (the seven Ps) is covered in 2.4 The target market; business models in 2.3 Business planning.

Porter's five forces

Pick an industry and read the evidence for each force. Then rate each force from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). The stronger the forces, the harder it is to make a profit, and the more a business needs a competitive advantage.

Industry

The suggested ratings are a starting point for discussion, written in general terms. There is no single right score: what matters is the evidence you give for each force.

1Which industry faces the strongest forces overall? Why do businesses still enter it?
2For the café, which force could Hilltop weaken most with a loyalty scheme?
3Why is the threat of new entrants low for smartphone makers but high for cafés?

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Name all five forces exactly: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of customers (buyers), threat of substitutes, competitive rivalry.
  2. For each force say high or low, give evidence from the business in the question, and say what it means for profit.
  3. Competitive advantage is why customers choose you over rivals: lower cost, a better or different product, a niche. Say how the business can capitalise on it.
  4. Growth strategies: market penetration, product development, market development, diversification; methods: organic growth, mergers and takeovers, alliances and franchising.
  5. Technology: link each tool to reach (new markets), efficiency (lower costs) or customer knowledge, and mention one risk.
  6. A cost-benefit analysis lists financial and non-financial costs and benefits, calculates net benefit and payback, and ends with a clear recommendation.
  7. Adapting: name the external driver (demographics, competition, economic), then the P (or business model) changed and why it helps financial sustainability.
  8. Show every formula and working: market share, growth %, payback, net benefit.
