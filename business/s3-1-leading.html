Strand 3 · Leading in business

Leaders set the direction.
Managers make it happen.
Culture decides how it feels.

Every organisation needs both leading and managing. Leaders give people a reason to follow; managers plan, organise and control so the work gets done. The way they lead shapes the culture, and the culture decides whether new ideas grow or die.

Labs

Sort leading from managing, control a sales plan, survey branches about culture, run an innovation funnel, choose a style for each situation and watch a year under four leaders.

Leading or managing?

Crann Outdoor (illustrative) is a Galway company with 40 staff that designs outdoor clothing and sells it online and in two shops. Sort what its people do into leading (direction, people, change) or managing (plans, systems, control).

Sorter

Crann Outdoor is illustrative. In real organisations the same person often leads and manages; the sorter asks which side of the job each activity shows best.

1Which words keep coming up in the leading column? In the managing column?
2Could a company be well managed but badly led? What would it feel like to work there?
3Think of a sports captain and a team manager. Who leads and who manages?
Plan, then control

Managers plan (set a target), organise (put people and resources in place) and control (compare results with the plan and act). Set Crann's monthly online sales target and the tolerance. Then run six months and see which months need action.

Results

Simulated data. Variance = actual − target; variance % = variance ÷ target × 100. A negative (adverse) variance in sales means the business sold less than planned.

1Narrow the tolerance to ±2%. How many months now need action? Is that useful or just noise?
2Raise the target to €80 000. What happens, and what does that say about setting realistic plans?
3For the worst month, suggest one corrective action a manager could take.
Culture and ideas: survey the branches

Does an open culture go with more innovation? Survey a branch of a large retail chain (illustrative): staff rate how open the culture is (1 to 5), and you record how many staff ideas were put into practice per 100 staff last year. Collect at least five branches, then look at the fitted line.

Data

Simulated data. A strong link (correlation) doesn't prove that culture causes innovation: successful branches might simply have more money and confident staff. You would also look at other evidence.

1After 5 branches, what is the slope? After 20? Why does it settle down?
2Is r close to 1? What does a weaker r tell you?
3Name two things a manager could change to make a branch's culture more open.
Innovation funnel

Most ideas never make it. Set how Crann supports innovation: paid time for staff to work on ideas, how mistakes are treated, and a budget for testing prototypes. Follow the ideas through the funnel and weigh the cost against the gain.

Illustrative model: 40 staff costing €40 000 a year each; each prototype test costs €4 000; each launch adds about €80 000 of sales a year. Real results vary much more, and many benefits (staff morale, reputation) don't show up in one year's figures.

1Set paid time to 0 and mistakes to "blamed". How many products are launched?
2Raise the budget but keep a blame culture. Why doesn't money alone fix it?
3Find the setting with the best return. Is the most ideas always best?
Which style fits?

There's no single best leadership style: it depends on the situation, the people and the time available. Pick a situation at Crann and a style. The bars show how well each style fits (illustrative scoring of speed, staff buy-in, creativity, control and driving change).

Situation
Your style

The scores are a model for discussion, not a rule. Good leaders adapt their style to the situation and often blend styles.

1In the fire evacuation, why does the democratic style score so badly?
2Which situation suits laissez-faire? What must be true of the team?
3Which style scores worst across every situation? Is any style never right?
A year under four leaders

How does a style build a culture, and the culture build innovation? Choose a style for Crann's new managing director and run a year. Watch staff engagement and the ideas suggested and put into practice. Then compare all four.

Style
Run

Simulated model for discussion. Engagement = how committed and involved staff feel (from a staff survey, 0 to 100).

1Which style gets the most ideas suggested? Why aren't they all put into practice?
2Why does engagement fall under the autocratic style even though work gets done?
3Run the same style twice. Why are the results a little different?

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. To distinguish, make the difference clear on both sides: leaders set direction, inspire and drive change; managers plan, organise and control resources to reach goals.
  2. Name the management activities: planning, organising, staffing, directing (leading) and controlling. Give an example for each from the business in the question.
  3. Define organisational culture as "the way we do things around here": shared values, beliefs, behaviours and norms. Give signs of it (how decisions are made, how mistakes are treated, dress, language, stories).
  4. For "analyse the significance", link culture and innovation to success: motivation and retention, reputation, attracting talent, adapting to change, new products and lower costs. Weigh the costs and risks too.
  5. Innovation can be in products, processes or business models, and can be incremental (small steps) or radical (new to the world). Name an innovative approach: intrapreneurship, design thinking, kaizen, suggestion schemes, open innovation, hackathons, R&D.
  6. Leadership styles: autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire and transformational. For each, say how it shapes the culture and whether it helps or blocks innovation, with an advantage and a disadvantage.
  7. The best style depends on the situation: urgency, the skills of the team, the size of the change.
  8. Show every formula and working: variance, variance %, labour turnover, idea conversion rate, return on investment.
