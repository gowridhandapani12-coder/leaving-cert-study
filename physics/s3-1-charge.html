Atoms, ions and charge

An atom has a tiny nucleus of protons (+) and neutrons (no charge), with electrons (−) around it. With equal numbers of protons and electrons it is neutral. Only electrons can be added or removed in everyday charging, so a charged object has gained or lost electrons.

Free or bound electrons?

Sorter

Simplified shell model (2, 8, 8, …) with the most common isotope of each element. Real atoms are about 10⁻¹⁰ m across and the nucleus is about 100 000 times smaller, so nothing here is to scale. e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

1Make a sodium ion with a charge of +1e. How many electrons does it have?
2Give oxygen two extra electrons. What is its charge in coulombs?
3Why can't you charge an object by adding protons to it?
Experiment: charging by friction

Rub two different neutral materials together and electrons move from one to the other. The one lower in the triboelectric series gains electrons and becomes negative; the other is left positive by exactly the same amount. Measure the rod's charge with a Faraday pail and coulombmeter for different numbers of strokes, then plot Q against strokes.

Forces between charged objects

Two small metal-coated balls hang on insulating threads. Give each one a charge. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract, and a charged ball attracts a neutral one by pulling its electrons to one side (polarisation). The forces on the two balls are always equal and opposite.

Attract, repel or no force?

Sorter

Charges are in units of 10 nC. The size of the swing is a qualitative model: the force grows with both charges and with how close the balls are (Coulomb's law, 3.2, gives the exact rule). The balls are conductors, so when they touch the charge is shared equally.

1Charge A and leave B neutral. Which way does B swing? Why?
2Now let them touch. What happens to the charges, and then to the balls?
3Give them +2 and −2 and let them touch. Where did the charge go?
Testing conductors and insulators

A charged gold-leaf electroscope has its leaves spread apart because they carry the same charge. Connect its cap to earth through a material. A conductor has free electrons that carry the charge away and the leaves fall at once; an insulator's electrons are bound to their atoms, so the leaves stay up.

Material between the cap and earth

Classify the materials

Sorter

Leaf angle is shown as a fraction of its starting value. Leak times are illustrative: metals discharge the electroscope almost instantly, your body and damp string in a second or two, good insulators not noticeably at all. Air is a very poor conductor when dry; damp air slowly discharges everything.

1Test every material. Sort them into two groups by what the leaves do.
2Touch the cap with your finger. Are you a conductor? Why do you not get a shock?
3Why can you charge a plastic rod by rubbing it while holding it, but not a metal rod?
Charging a single sphere by induction

You can charge a metal sphere on an insulating stand without touching it with the charged rod. Bring the rod near, earth the sphere, remove the earth, then remove the rod. The sphere ends up with the opposite charge to the rod, and the rod keeps all its own charge.

Charge on the rod

Put the steps in order

Sorter

Only electrons move. The positive charges (protons in the nuclei) stay where they are. "Earthing" or "grounding" means connecting to the Earth, a huge conductor that can give or take any number of electrons.

1Go through all four steps with a negative rod. Where do the electrons go when you earth the sphere?
2Repeat with a positive rod. Which way do electrons flow through the earth wire now?
3What goes wrong if you remove the rod before removing the earth?
Where does the charge go?

On a conductor the charges repel each other and spread out over the outside surface, crowding most where the surface curves most sharply. On an insulator the charge stays where it was put. Pick a shape, then touch a proof plane to points on it and read the charge it picks up.

Free and bound electrons

Conductor

Metals have free electrons that drift between the fixed positive ions, so charge flows through them easily.

Insulator

Every electron is bound to its own atom. Charge put on an insulator stays where it is.

Polarisation

Near a charged rod the electrons in each atom shift slightly. The near side becomes oppositely charged, so the object is attracted.

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Objects become charged by gaining or losing electrons. Protons never move in charging.
  2. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract, and a charged object attracts a neutral one by polarising it.
  3. Only repulsion proves an object is charged: attraction could be to a neutral object.
  4. Conductors have free electrons; in insulators the electrons are bound to their atoms.
  5. Charge is conserved: after rubbing, the two charges are equal and opposite and add to zero.
  6. Induction in four steps: rod near → earth → remove earth → remove rod. The sphere gets the opposite charge.
  7. On a conductor charge sits on the outside surface and is densest at sharp points.
  8. Identical conductors that touch share the total charge equally: (Q₁ + Q₂) / 2 each.
