Strand 1 · Section 1.6 · Uniform circular motion

Swing it.
Spin it.
Orbit it.

Anything moving in a circle is accelerating towards the centre, so it needs a centripetal force. Measure angular velocity, whirl a bung to find a hidden mass, check Kepler's third law with real data, and see how gravity keeps the Moon, the planets and satellites in their orbits.

Students learn about:

  • the centripetal force required to maintain uniform motion in a circle
  • evidence that the force of gravity meets the centripetal force requirements for planetary motion
  • relate the orbits of satellites to their uses

Equations in the specification for 1.6: F = mv²/r and T² = 4π²R³/GM. This page also uses ω = θ/t, v = ωr, a = v²/r = ω²r and T = 2π/ω to model motion with constant angular velocity. Builds on 1.1 (velocity and acceleration), 1.2 (F = ma) and 1.5 (F = Gm₁m₂/d²).

Centripetal force

A ball on a string moves in a horizontal circle (seen from above). Its velocity keeps changing direction, so it accelerates towards the centre. The string pulls it inwards with the centripetal force F = mv²/r. Cut the string and watch where it goes.

What provides the centripetal force?

Sorter

The string snaps if the tension needed is more than 60 N. Air resistance and the weight of the ball are ignored (a smooth horizontal table).

1Double the speed. What happens to the force? Double the radius instead. What happens now?
2Cut the string. Does the ball fly outwards or along a tangent? Why?
3Find the fastest speed the string can take for a 1 kg ball on a 0.5 m string.
Model: constant angular velocity

A disc spins at a constant angular velocity ω, in radians per second. Every point on it turns through the same angle, but points further out move faster: v = ωr. Watch the angle in radians build up and compare an inner and an outer point.

Point A is fixed 0.10 m from the centre. One radian is the angle where the arc length equals the radius: 2π rad = 360°.

1Pause when the angle is close to π rad. How many degrees is that?
2Point B is four times as far out as A. How do their speeds compare? Their angular velocities?
3Double ω. What happens to T, to v and to the centripetal force on B?
Experiment: the whirling bung

A rubber bung of unknown mass m is whirled on a string through a glass tube. Hanging masses on the other end set the tension, F = Mg, which is the centripetal force. Keep r fixed with a marker, time 10 revolutions, and work out v = 2πr/T. Plot F against v²: the slope is m/r.

Kepler's third law from secondary data

For bodies orbiting the same central mass, T² is proportional to R³. Gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/R² = m(2πR/T)²/R, so T² = (4π²/GM) R³. Pick a real data set, plot T² against R³, check for a straight line through the origin, and use the slope to find the central mass.

Data set

Secondary data: mean orbital radii and periods (rounded published values). Orbits are treated as circles; G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². Animation speeds are scaled so you can see them.

1Work out T²/R³ for each row. What do you notice?
2Why does each data set give a different slope?
3Which data set gives the mass of the Sun? Of Jupiter? Of the Earth?
Gravity provides the centripetal force

Newton imagined a cannon on a very high mountain. Fire a 1000 kg probe sideways from 400 km above the Earth. Too slow and it falls back; at just the right speed gravity gives exactly the centripetal force a circle needs, so it falls around the Earth for ever.

Try

Model: no air resistance, Earth treated as a uniform sphere (M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m), the Earth's rotation is ignored. Paths are worked out step by step from F = GMm/r².

1Fire at 6 km/s and at 7 km/s. Compare gravity with the centripetal force needed at launch.
2Find the speed for a circular orbit. Why does the 1000 kg mass not matter?
3Above which speed does the probe never come back? Compare with 1.5's escape velocity.
Satellite orbits and their uses

The higher the orbit, the slower the satellite and the longer its period: T² = 4π²r³/GM. At about 36 000 km up the period is 24 hours, so a geostationary satellite stays above the same point on the equator. Pick an orbit and watch it next to the spinning Earth.

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Centripetal force is not a new kind of force. Name what provides it: tension, friction, gravity or a normal reaction.
  2. The force and acceleration point towards the centre; the velocity is along the tangent.
  3. Angles in ω = θ/t are in radians. One revolution = 2π rad, so ω = 2π/T.
  4. Use F = mv²/r or F = mω²r: square the speed, and use metres for r.
  5. To derive T² = 4π²R³/GM, set GMm/R² = mv²/R and use v = 2πR/T.
  6. For Kepler's third law, plot T² against R³: straight line through the origin, slope 4π²/GM.
  7. For satellites, r = R + h from the centre of the Earth, in metres.
  8. Geostationary: period 24 h, above the equator, same direction as the Earth's spin, about 36 000 km up.
