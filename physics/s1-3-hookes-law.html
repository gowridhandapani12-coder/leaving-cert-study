Strand 1 · Section 1.3 · Stretching and compressing objects

Stretch it.
Squash it.
Let it spring back.

Springs, bungee cords, car suspensions and rubber bands all push or pull back when you change their length. Find out when that force is proportional to the stretch (Hooke's law), measure a spring constant, and see what happens past the elastic limit.

Model: F = −ks

A block on a smooth track is fixed to a spring. Pull it right (stretch) or push it left (compress). The spring's restoring force always points back towards the natural length.

The block has a mass of 0.50 kg and the track has no friction, so once you let go it keeps moving back and forth (you'll meet this again in 1.4 as an energy story).

1Set s = +0.08 m, then −0.08 m. What changes about the force, and what stays the same?

3Let go. Where is the force biggest? Where is it zero?

Graphs to know

F ∝ extension

A straight line through the origin is Hooke's law. The steeper line is the stiffer spring: slope = k.

P, E and beyond

Straight up to the limit of proportionality (P). Past the elastic limit (E) the stretch is permanent.

F = −ks

Stretch it (+s) and the force pulls back (−F). Compress it (−s) and it pushes out (+F).

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Extension = new length − natural length. Subtract before you use Hooke's law.
  2. Convert cm and mm to metres first, so k comes out in N/m.
  3. A hanging mass gives a force W = mg: 100 g gives 0.98 N.
  4. Plot force against extension (not length). A straight line through the origin verifies Hooke's law; the slope is k.
  5. In F = −ks the minus sign means the force is opposite to the displacement. Give the size and say which way.
  6. Hooke's law holds only up to the limit of proportionality. Past the elastic limit the stretch is permanent.
  7. Springs side by side (parallel) share the load; springs end to end (series) each carry the full load.
  8. Describe an experiment with: apparatus, what you measure, how, the graph, and one precaution.
