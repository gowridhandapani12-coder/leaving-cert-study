Strand 1 · Section 1.5 · Forces acting in a gravitational field

Gravity pulls every mass towards every other mass. Measure g four different ways, use Newton's law of universal gravitation to find g anywhere around a planet, and work out how fast a rocket must go to leave for good.

Experiment: g by free fall

An electromagnet holds a steel ball a height s above a trapdoor. Switching it off starts the timer; the ball hits the trapdoor and stops it. From rest, s = ½gt², so g = 2s/t². Drop from five or more heights and plot s against t²: the slope is ½g.

Weight and g = F/m

Hang masses on a newton balance on four different worlds. The balance reads the weight F. The force on each kilogram, F/m, is the gravitational field strength g. Your mass stays the same everywhere; your weight doesn't.

Newton's law of universal gravitation

Every mass attracts every other mass. F = Gm₁m₂/d²: double one mass and the force doubles; double the distance and it drops to a quarter. Pick a pair, then scale the masses and the distance.

Pair

G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². d is measured between the centres of the two masses. Arrow lengths show the force compared with the starting pair (very long arrows are capped).

1Set d × 2, then d × 3. What fraction of the starting force is left each time?
2Can you find settings that give the same force as the start, with both masses changed?
3The Earth pulls you with about 600 N. How hard do you pull the Earth?
Model: g at any point in a field

Outside a planet, g = GM/r², where r is the distance from the planet's centre. At the surface r = R, so g = GM/R². Move the probe away from the planet and watch g fall as an inverse square.

Data: Moon M = 7.35 × 10²² kg, R = 1.74 × 10⁶ m; Mars 6.42 × 10²³ kg, 3.39 × 10⁶ m; Earth 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, 6.37 × 10⁶ m; Jupiter 1.90 × 10²⁷ kg, 6.99 × 10⁷ m. Planets are treated as uniform spheres; the slider goes up to 6 radii above the surface (r = 7R).

Escape velocity

Fire a probe straight up (no air, no engine after launch). Too slow and gravity pulls it back. At the escape velocity vₑ = √(2GM/r) its kinetic energy is just enough to get infinitely far away.

Launch from

Model: the probe moves straight up from the surface with no air resistance and no engine after launch; the planet's rotation is ignored. The slider sets the launch speed as 10–130% of that body's escape velocity.

1Launch at 70% of vₑ, then 90%. How much higher does the probe go?
2Does a heavier probe need a bigger escape velocity? Check with ½mv² = GMm/r.
3Why is it so much easier to leave the Moon than the Earth?

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Know all four models for g: 2s/t² (free fall), 4π²l/T² (pendulum), F/m (weight per kg) and GM/R² (from the planet's mass and radius).
  2. In g = GM/r², r is measured from the centre of the planet: add the planet's radius to the height above the surface.
  3. Square the distance, and convert km to m before you substitute. Most wrong answers here are off by a power of 10.
  4. Inverse square shortcuts: 2 × the distance → ¼ of the force, 3 × → 1/9, 10 × → 1/100.
  5. Mass is in kg and never changes; weight is a force in N and depends on g (W = mg).
  6. For graphs, say what you plot and what the slope means: s v t² has slope ½g; T² v l has slope 4π²/g.
  7. Escape velocity doesn't depend on the mass of the rocket: m cancels in ½mv² = GMm/r.
  8. Gravity is always attractive, and the forces on the two masses are equal and opposite (Newton's third law).
