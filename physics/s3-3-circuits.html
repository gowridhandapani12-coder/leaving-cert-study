Strand 3 · Section 3.3 · Electric circuits

Push the charge.
Measure the flow.
Spend the energy safely.

A cell pushes charge round a circuit; every coulomb carries energy from the cell to the components. Current is the rate of flow of charge, potential difference is the energy per coulomb, and resistance decides how much current flows. Measure I–V graphs for a wire, a bulb and a diode, heat a wire and a thermistor, build series and parallel circuits, keep mains electricity safe and look inside the semiconductors in every phone.

Charge, current and energy

A cell drives charge round a loop through a resistor. Change the emf and the resistance and watch the charges speed up or slow down. Every coulomb that passes collects energy in the cell and gives it to the resistor.

Show

The cell is treated as ideal (no internal resistance), so the pd across the resistor equals the emf. Electron charge e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

1Double the emf. What happens to the current and to the energy delivered in 10 s?
2How many electrons pass a point each second when I = 0.5 A?
3Switch to electron flow. Why do the two arrows point opposite ways?
Experiment: I–V characteristics

Connect a component in the circuit, set the voltage with the variable supply, and read the ammeter and voltmeter. Take readings across the whole range (reverse the supply for negative values), then look at the shape of the I–V graph. A straight line through the origin means the component obeys Ohm's law.

Series and parallel

Connect three resistors in series, in parallel, or as R₁ in series with R₂ and R₃ in parallel. Read the current through each resistor and the pd across it. Check the two conservation laws: currents into a junction equal currents out (charge), and the pds round a loop add up to the emf (energy).

Arrangement

Ideal cell and meters. "Measure total resistance" simulates an ohmmeter reading (±1%) so you can compare your primary data with the formula.

1In parallel, is the total resistance bigger or smaller than the smallest resistor? Always?
2In series, which resistor has the biggest pd across it? Why?
3In the mixed circuit, add the currents in R₂ and R₃. What do you get?
Power, cost and mains safety

Pick an appliance on the 230 V mains. Find its current, the right fuse and the cost of running it. Then cause a fault, a live wire touching the metal case, and see what the earth wire, the fuse, the MCB and the RCD do.

Appliance
Safety (tap to switch)

Typical power ratings. Plug fuses come in 3 A, 5 A and 13 A; choose the smallest that is above the normal current. An RCD trips when live and neutral currents differ by about 30 mA, in about 30 ms.

1Which appliances need a 13 A fuse? Why would a 3 A fuse blow?
2Cause the fault with the earth connected, then disconnect it. What changes for the person?
3Why does an RCD protect a person even when the fuse doesn't blow?
Semiconductors: junction and switch

Part 1: bias a p-n junction and watch the depletion layer. Forward bias above about 0.6 V shrinks it and current flows; reverse bias widens it. Part 2: use an LDR and a transistor as a switch to build a night light that turns on when it gets dark.

Simplified models. LDR resistance falls as light rises (here about 500 kΩ in the dark to about 100 Ω in bright sun). A silicon transistor switches on when its base-emitter voltage reaches about 0.7 V.

1Slowly dim the light. At what light level does the LED switch on?
2Increase the fixed resistor. Does the light come on earlier or later in the evening?
3Swap the idea round: how could a thermistor switch on a fan when it gets hot?

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Current is the rate of flow of charge: I = Q/t. Potential difference is the work done per unit charge: V = W/Q.
  2. State Ohm's law fully: current is proportional to pd provided the temperature stays constant.
  3. For an I–V experiment, draw the circuit: ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel with the component, and a way to vary the voltage.
  4. Series: same current, pds add, R = R₁ + R₂. Parallel: same pd, currents add, 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂. Don't forget to flip 1/R at the end.
  5. R = ρL/A: use A = πd²/4 and convert mm to m (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²).
  6. Power: P = VI = I²R. Energy: W = Pt = I²Rt. For cost, use kW × hours = kWh.
  7. Safety: the fuse and MCB protect the wiring from too much current; the RCD protects people; the earth wire gives fault current a safe path.
  8. Diode: conducts in forward bias above about 0.6 V; almost no current in reverse bias (the depletion layer widens).
