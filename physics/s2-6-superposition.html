Strand 2 · Section 2.6 · Principle of superposition

Two waves meet.
They add up.
The string sings.

When waves overlap, their displacements add. Send two waves along a string in opposite directions and you get a stationary wave: nodes that never move, loops that do, and the harmonics that make music.

Students learn about:

  • stationary waves in strings as resulting from the interference of two waves along the same string moving in opposite directions
  • common terms used to describe stationary waves: nodes, antinodes, harmonics and the fundamental frequency

Stationary and standing wave mean the same thing. Travelling waves (v = fλ) are section 2.2; sound interference was met in 2.5.

Harmonics · LO 2.6.1

Six harmonics on one guitar string

The A string of a guitar: L = 0.65 m and f₁ = 110 Hz. Each pattern is one harmonic.

The principle of superposition

When pulses meet

Crest meets crest

Constructive: the displacements add to make one bigger pulse.

Crest meets trough

Destructive: equal and opposite pulses cancel for an instant.

After they meet

Each pulse carries on unchanged, as if the other was never there.

Secondary data · LO 2.6.3

Frets on a guitar: shorter string, higher note

Pressing a fret shortens the vibrating length of the A string. Look at the last column: f × L stays the same, so f ∝ 1/L.

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Write the formula first, then substitute, then the answer with its unit.
  2. Superposition: the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements (signs matter).
  3. Stationary wave = two identical waves travelling in opposite directions along the same string.
  4. Node to node = λ/2. Two loops make one wavelength.
  5. Count loops for the harmonic number: n loops, n + 1 nodes, n antinodes, λ = 2L/n.
  6. Convert to SI first: g/m × 10⁻³ = kg/m, cm × 10⁻² = m.
  7. f ∝ 1/L, f ∝ √T, f ∝ 1/√μ. Quadruple T to double f.
  8. Sonometer graph: plot f against 1/L for a straight line through the origin.
