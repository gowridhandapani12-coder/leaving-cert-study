Strand 3 · Section 3.2 · Modelling electric fields

Push without touching.
Map the field.
Follow the lines.

Charges push and pull each other across empty space. Coulomb's law tells you how hard: double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. The electric field is the model that explains it: every charge fills the space around it with a field, and any other charge placed there feels a force. Measure the inverse square law, probe a field with a test charge, draw field lines, add field vectors, push a charge through a potential difference and steer an ink drop.

Experiment: Coulomb's law

Two small charged spheres sit a distance d apart (centre to centre). Set their charges, move them apart, and measure the force with a sensitive balance. Record the force at five or more distances, then plot F against 1/d². A straight line through the origin shows the inverse square law.

No readings yet. Measure the force at five or more distances.

Simulated balance readings with about ±3% scatter. Charges are in nanocoulombs (1 nC = 10⁻⁹ C); the spheres are small enough to treat as point charges. Changing a charge clears the table. ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, so 1/4πε₀ ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻².

1Halve the distance. By what factor does the force change?
2Make one charge negative. What happens to the direction of the forces? To their size?
3Use the slope of your graph to find a value for ε₀.
Probe the field with a test charge

A source charge Q creates an electric field. Place a small positive test charge q at a distance r and measure the force on it. Change q: the force changes, but the force per unit charge, E = F/q, stays the same. E belongs to the field, not to the test charge.

The test charge is kept small so it doesn't disturb the source charge. E is a vector: its direction is the direction of the force on a positive test charge.

1Double q. What happens to F? To E?
2Double r. What happens to E? Why is this an inverse square law?
3Make Q negative. Which way does E point now?
Field lines

Field lines show the direction of the force on a positive test charge. They start on positive charges and end on negative ones, never cross, and are closest together where the field is strongest. Pick an arrangement, then tap anywhere on the field to measure its strength and direction there.

Arrangement

Field line rules: true or false?

Sorter

Lines are traced from the computed field of point charges; the plates are modelled as rows of point charges, so you can see the uniform field in the middle and the curved "edge effect" at the ends. Strength is shown relative to the strongest point probed.

1For "+ and −", probe near a charge and then far away. Where are the lines densest?
2For "+ and +", find the point between them where the field is zero.
3Probe several points between the plates. What is special about the field there?
Adding field vectors

Electric field strength is a vector. At a point P, each charge makes its own field, pointing away from a positive charge and towards a negative one. The total field is the vector sum. Move P and change the charges.

Presets

A is at (−5 cm, 0) and B at (+5 cm, 0). Charges in nC. Arrow lengths are scaled so you can see both; the readouts give the true values in N C⁻¹.

1At the midpoint of + and −, do the fields add or cancel? Why?
2At the midpoint of two equal + charges, what is the resultant?
3Put P above the middle. Which components cancel and which add?
Work, energy and potential

Between two parallel plates the field is uniform. Push a positive charge from the negative plate towards the positive plate, against the force of the field. The work you do per coulomb is the potential difference: V = W/q.

Plates 10 cm apart. Charges in µC (10⁻⁶ C). The negative plate is taken as 0 V. Work done against the field is stored as electric potential energy and is released if you let go.

1Double q. What happens to the work done? To V?
2How much work does it take to move 1 C all the way across? Compare with the plate voltage.
3Where does the energy go when you let the charge go?
Steer a charged ink drop

In a continuous ink-jet printer, tiny drops are given a charge and fly between two deflecting plates. The field exerts a force F = qE on each drop, which bends its path up or down. Set the charge and field to hit the target on the paper.

Drop mass 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ kg, speed 20 m s⁻¹, plates 1.0 cm long, paper 2.0 cm beyond the plates. The field points downwards (top plate positive). Gravity is ignored: mg ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁹ N, about a thousand times smaller than a typical electric force here. Charges in pC (10⁻¹² C), field in kN C⁻¹.

1Which way does a negative drop bend in a downward field? Why?
2Double the charge. What happens to the force, the acceleration and the deflection?
3Hit three targets in a row without changing the field.

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Coulomb's law: F = Q₁Q₂ / 4πε₀d². Convert nC and µC to C and cm to m before you substitute.
  2. Inverse square: double d → F ÷ 4; triple d → F ÷ 9. Double one charge → F × 2.
  3. The forces on the two charges are equal and opposite (Newton's third law), even if the charges are different.
  4. Define E precisely: the force per unit charge on a small positive test charge placed at that point. Unit N C⁻¹.
  5. E is a vector: give its direction (away from +, towards −) and add fields as vectors, drawing a diagram.
  6. Field lines: start on +, end on −, never cross, closer together = stronger field, and meet conductors at right angles.
  7. Potential: V = W/q. 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb.
  8. Compare with gravity: both are inverse square, but electric forces can attract or repel and are far stronger.
