Strand 3 · Section 3.4 · Magnetic fields

Feel the pull.
Follow the lines.

Magnets and electric currents both fill the space around them with a magnetic field. Map the field of one magnet and two magnets close together, measure how the field round a wire falls with distance, make a solenoid stronger with an iron core, watch two currents pull together, steer charged particles into circles with F = Bqv, and find out where permanent and temporary magnets are used.

Students learn about:

  • Magnetic fields around permanent and temporary magnets
  • the interaction of magnetic fields
  • magnetic effect of an electric current
  • force exerted by a magnetic field on a moving charge
  • the effect of a ferrous core on the magnetic field around a solenoid

Equation in the specification for 3.4: F = Bqv. Builds on 3.1 to 3.3 (charge and current) and 1.6 (circular motion); leads on to 3.5 (the force on a current-carrying conductor and the motor) and 3.6 (induction and the generator). B ∝ I/r for a wire and B ∝ nI for a solenoid are used here to model relative field strength.

Two currents side by side

Two long parallel wires seen end on. Set each current (positive = out of the page) and the gap between them. Watch how the two fields combine, find the neutral point, and see whether the wires attract or repel.

Quick set

Long straight wires in air. The force per metre uses F/L = μ₀I₁I₂/2πd, which is not one of the spec equations: what matters is the pattern, F ∝ I₁I₂ and F ∝ 1/d.

1Same direction: where is the field zero? Is the field stronger between the wires or outside them?
2Switch to opposite directions. Where is the field strongest now? Which way do the wires move?
3Double both currents. How many times bigger is the force?
Force on a moving charge

Fire an electron, a proton or an alpha particle into a uniform magnetic field at right angles. The force F = Bqv is always at right angles to the velocity, so the particle curves. Change the speed, the field and its direction.

Particle
Field direction

Charges: e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (alpha: 2e). Masses: electron 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, proton 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, alpha 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. The field region is 2 cm wide for electrons and 1 m wide for protons and alphas. Speeds well below the speed of light.

1Double B. What happens to the force and to the radius of the path?
2Switch from a proton to an electron at the same speed. Which way does it curve, and why is its circle so much smaller?
3The speed never changes. Why not, when there is a force on the particle?

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Outside a magnet, field lines go from N to S. They never cross, and closer lines mean a stronger field.
  2. Between like poles, mark the neutral point, where the fields cancel.
  3. Round a straight wire the lines are concentric circles. Use the right-hand grip rule for their direction. B ∝ I and B ∝ 1/r.
  4. Solenoid: uniform field inside, bar-magnet pattern outside. Seen from an end, anticlockwise current means that end is N.
  5. A soft iron core makes the field hundreds of times stronger, until the iron saturates.
  6. F = Bqv when v is at right angles to B. F = 0 if the charge is at rest or moves along the field.
  7. Fleming's left-hand rule: Field, Current, Motion. For an electron, the current is opposite to its motion.
  8. The force on a moving charge is perpendicular to its velocity: no work is done, the speed stays the same and the path is a circle.
