Strand 1 · Section 1.4 · A work-energy model for analysing particle motion

Push it.
Lift it.
Follow the energy.

Every time a force moves something, work is done and energy changes form: height into speed, speed into heat, a stretched spring into a flying ball. Learn to calculate work, kinetic and potential energy and power, then use the principle of conservation of energy to predict what happens next.

Students learn about (several of these appear as equations in the specification):

  • work done: W = Fs
  • gravitational potential energy: EP = mgh
  • kinetic energy: EK = ½mv²
  • power, the rate of doing work: P = W/t
  • work done in stretching or compressing: EP = ½ks²
  • the principle of conservation of energy

Builds on 1.2 (forces, friction, W = mg) and 1.3 (Hooke's law, F = −ks). Energy comes back in 2.1 (heat), 1.5 (gravity) and 4.5 (E = mc²).

Elastic potential energy

Pull back a spring launcher and let it go. The work done compressing the spring is the area under its force–compression graph, stored as ½ks², and becomes the ball's kinetic energy.

Smooth, level track: all the stored elastic energy becomes kinetic energy. Hooke's law (1.3) is assumed to hold for every compression on the slider.

1Double the compression. How many times more energy is stored? How much faster is the launch?
2Keep the energy the same and make the ball four times heavier. What happens to the speed?
3Why is the energy ½ks² and not ks × s?

Experiment: conservation of energy

Release a ball (or a trolley on a ramp) from a measured height h. A light gate at the bottom measures its speed v. If all of mgh becomes ½mv², then v² = 2gh: plot v² against h and compare the slope with 2g = 19.6 m/s².

Simulated light-gate speeds with a small random error (about 1%). Ball and trolley mass 0.50 kg. "New trolley" hides a new amount of friction; the dropped ball loses almost nothing to air resistance over 1 m. The secondary data set is a sample table made up for practice.

1Dropped ball: is the slope close to 19.6? What does that tell you?
2Trolley: the slope is smaller. What fraction of EP became EK? Where did the rest go?
3Why plot v² (not v) against h?
Model: a roller coaster

A 500 kg car starts at rest at the top of the first hill. Follow its energy along the track. Change the hill heights and the resistance (friction and air) and see whether it clears the second hill.

Model: the resistance is a steady backwards force per kilogram, so the heat produced is m × resistance × distance travelled. g = 9.8 m/s².

1Set the resistance to 0. What is the highest second hill the car can clear?
2Does a heavier car reach a different speed at the bottom? Check with mgh = ½mv².
3Add resistance. Where does the "missing" energy go? Is the total still the same?

Graphs to know

EK ∝ v²

Kinetic energy grows with the square of speed: twice as fast, four times the energy (and four times the braking distance).

Area = work done

The area under a force–extension line is the work done. For a spring it is a triangle: ½ks².

The total stays the same

Along a roller coaster, EP and EK swap back and forth while heat slowly grows. Their sum is constant.

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Work is done only when a force moves something in its own direction. No movement, or movement at right angles: no work.
  2. Use SI units: mass in kg, height and distance in m, time in s. Energy comes out in joules.
  3. In EK = ½mv², square v first. Twice the speed means four times the energy.
  4. Measure h from a reference level (usually the lowest point). Only changes in height matter.
  5. EP = ½ks² comes from the area of a triangle under the F–s line. Use s in metres.
  6. Power is a rate: P = W/t. Same work in half the time = twice the power.
  7. For conservation questions, write energy at the start = energy at the end (+ heat), then cancel m if you can.
  8. Never say energy is "used up" or "lost": say it is transferred to heat and sound (dissipated).
