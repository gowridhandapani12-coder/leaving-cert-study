Bell jar

An electric bell rings inside a glass jar. Pump the air out and listen. Turn the sound on to hear it fade.

Pump

In a real jar you never hear perfect silence: a little sound still travels through the wires holding the bell and through the base plate. Those are solids, so they are a medium too.

1Pump the air out. Can you still see the hammer moving?
2What does that tell you about light compared with sound?
3Let the air back in. Does the sound come back?
Particle model

A loudspeaker pushes and pulls the air. Watch the red particle: it vibrates back and forth, but it doesn't travel with the wave.

Animation (slowed right down)
1Pause it. Point to a compression and a rarefaction.
2Raise the frequency. What happens to the wavelength?
3Does the red particle ever reach the right-hand side?
Two loudspeakers

Two speakers play the same note. Walk along the line and find loud and quiet spots. Only waves can cancel out like this.

Sound

Interference is part of section 2.6 (superposition). Here it is evidence that sound is a wave: particles can't cancel each other out, but waves can.

1Find a quiet spot. What is the path difference in wavelengths?
2Raise the frequency. Do the loud spots get closer together?
3Turn the sound on and slide slowly through a quiet spot.
Experiment

Stand in front of a tall wall. Clap and time the echo at different distances. Plot 2d against t: the slope is the speed of sound.

Frequency ranges

What can you hear?

Infrasound: below 20 Hz

Too low for us. Elephants, whales and earthquakes make it.

Audible: 20 Hz to 20 kHz

Human hearing. The top limit falls as you get older.

Ultrasound: above 20 kHz

Too high for us. Bats, dolphins and medical scanners use it.

Top exam tips for this chapter

  1. Write the formula first, then substitute, then the answer with its unit.
  2. Sound is a mechanical, longitudinal wave. It needs a medium; light doesn't.
  3. Bell jar: say what you hear (quieter), what you see (hammer still moving) and the conclusion.
  4. Pitch ↔ frequency. Loudness ↔ amplitude. Don't mix them up.
  5. Echo questions: the sound goes there and back, so the distance is 2d: v = 2d / t.
  6. Convert first: ms × 10⁻³, μs × 10⁻⁶, kHz × 10³, MHz × 10⁶.
  7. Ultrasound means frequency above 20 kHz. For depth use d = vt / 2.
  8. For a use of ultrasound, name it, say what it detects, and why ultrasound suits it (e.g. non-ionising).
