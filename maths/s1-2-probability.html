    Labs

    Roll dice, spin a mystery spinner, shade Venn diagrams, draw from a bag, test a population and design a fair game. Every result updates live.

    Two dice

    The sample space for rolling two dice has 36 equally likely outcomes. Pick an event: its outcomes light up and P(E) = favourable ÷ total.

    Event E
    Show in cells
    1Which total is most likely? Count the cells for each sum.
    2"At least one 6": why is it 11 cells, not 12?
    3Check P(E) + P(not E) = 1 for three different events.
    Collect & estimate

    Repeat an experiment many times. The relative frequency (hits ÷ trials) wobbles at first, then settles near the probability. Estimate the hidden probability of the mystery spinner from your data.

    Experiment
    Trials
    1Do 10 trials of the die, reset and do 10 again. Why are the answers so different?
    2Mystery spinner: after 1 000 trials, what's your estimate? Then reveal it.
    3Expected number of hits = trials × probability. Check it against your count.
    Venn diagram

    A class of 30: A = plays a sport, B = plays an instrument. Set the numbers in each region, then pick a region to shade and find its probability.

    Shade
    1Shade A ∪ B. Why do you subtract the overlap when you add P(A) and P(B)?
    2Set "Both" to 0. What does mutually exclusive look like?
    3Shade A′ and A. Do their probabilities always add to 1?
    Bag and tree

    Take two sweets from a bag, with or without putting the first one back. The tree shows the probability on every branch: multiply along a path, add the paths you want.

    Sampling
    Event
    Check by experiment
    1Switch between with and without replacement. Which branches change, and why?
    2Find "at least one red" two ways: add three paths, or 1 − P(both blue).
    3With replacement, is P(2nd red | 1st red) equal to P(2nd red)? That's independence.
    Screening test

    1 000 people take a test for a rare condition. Change how common it is and how good the test is. Compare P(positive | condition) with P(condition | positive).

    1With the defaults, most positives are false alarms. Count them on the grid.
    2Raise "has the condition" to 20%. What happens to P(condition | positive)?
    3Which matters more here: a better test, or a lower false positive rate?
    Design a game

    Pay to roll a fair die. A 6 wins one prize, a 5 wins a smaller prize, anything else wins nothing. Find the expected winnings, then set the price so the game is fair.

    Play
    1The expected winnings are €1.50, but can you ever win exactly €1.50 in one game?
    2Find the cost that makes the game fair. Play 1 000 games to check.
    3You run the stall at a fundraiser. What price do you choose, and why?

    Top exam tips for probability

    1. Write the sample space (a grid, a list or a tree) when the outcomes are equally likely, then count.
    2. Every probability is between 0 and 1. An answer of 1.3 or −0.2 means something went wrong.
    3. "Not": use P(A′) = 1 − P(A). "At least one" is usually 1 − P(none).
    4. "Or": add, then subtract the overlap: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
    5. "And": multiply, but only use P(A) × P(B) if A and B are independent. Otherwise use P(B | A).
    6. Without replacement: the second branch has one fewer in the total (and maybe the top).
    7. Read "given" carefully: P(A | B) is not P(B | A). The thing after the bar is what you already know.
    8. Expected value = Σ x × P(x). A game is fair when the expected gain is 0. Questions here are practice questions, not official SEC papers.

    Probability on one page. The rules are given in the syllabus; check which ones are in your Formulae and Tables booklet.

    Equally likelyP(E) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes
    Range
    0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
    Sum of 7 with two dice: 6/36 = 1/6
    ExperimentRelative frequency = hits ÷ trials
    Expected count
    trials × P(E)
    600 rolls: expect 600 × 1/6 = 100 sixes
    NotP(A′) = 1 − P(A)
    At least one
    1 − P(none)
    Addition ruleP(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
    0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7
    Mutually exclusiveP(A ∩ B) = 0, so P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)
    Venn
    circles don't overlap
    Multiplication (independent)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
    Two sixes: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36
    Multiplication (general)P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
    Two reds from 5R 3B, no replacement: 5/8 × 4/7 = 5/14
    ConditionalP(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(A)
    Careful
    in general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A)
    IndependentP(B | A) = P(B)
    Means
    knowing A doesn't change the chance of B
    Expected valueE(X) = Σ x · P(x)
    €6 × 1/6 + €3 × 1/6 = €1.50
    Fair gameExpected gain = 0
    So
    cost to play = expected winnings
    Tree diagramsMultiply along a path, add the paths
    Check
    all the end probabilities add to 1
